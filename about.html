    Background

    The database is built by Andreas Rydström, a registered dietitian based in Uppsala, Sweden. It grew out of a practical problem: what is known about which foods carry which properties sits scattered across Monash listings, allergen legislation, national nutrition tables and individual studies, in different units and behind different thresholds. This is an attempt to lay them side by side in a form that can be looked things up in, with every threshold written down and every figure traceable to its source.

    Everything is worked out in the browser and nothing is sent anywhere — a selection stays on the device between visits and never reaches a server. Adding the site to a home screen as an app, and using it with no connection, are in the full version.

    Why this tool exists

    The properties that matter for food intolerance work — FODMAP subtypes, histamine content, allergens, cross-reactivity groups — sit scattered across separate references: Monash listings, allergen legislation, national nutrition tables, individual studies. Cross-referencing a list of foods against all of them by hand, property by property, is slow and easy to get wrong. This tool automates that lookup, comparing many foods against many properties at once and surfacing what they have in common — a faster way to consult the same references, not a substitute for them.

    How the analysis works

    Every food in the tool is tagged with the traits it carries — FODMAP subtypes, histamine content, allergens, GI irritant mechanisms, and more. When you select a group of foods, the tool counts how many of them share each trait, and ranks the traits by how common they are across your selection. What comes out is a shortlist of what your foods have in common.

    Traits are measured per portion

    Some traits depend on how much of something a food contains. Those are measured as an amount in one normal portion, not as a figure per 100 grams — because a figure per 100 grams says nothing until you know how much goes on a plate. The same 17.5g of fat per 100g is about 4g in a slice of cheese and about 22g in a portion of mince.

    • Fat: 6.1g in a portion
    • Protein: 15g in a portion
    • Fiber: 6.1g in a portion
    • Lactose: 5g in a portion
    • Bile stimulant: 9.5g of fat in a portion, counting protein at a fifth of its weight, since protein triggers the same hormone far more weakly
    • Salicylates: 1mg in a portion
    • Alcohol: above 0.5% by volume — a concentration, because that is the legal line for calling a drink alcohol-free

    Every food therefore carries a typical serving in grams. Servings come from the food group, so that foods eaten the same way get the same number: every fruit 100g, every berry 50g, every cut of meat, fish and poultry 125g, potato and cooked grains 175g, a slice of bread 40g, a slice of cheese 20g.

    That cuts both ways, and it is worth being plain about it. A serving is what a typical plate holds, not what your plate holds. Eat half of one and a trait shown here may not reach you; eat two and a trait that stays hidden may. The numbers are deliberately not padded to cover the larger helping — a food flagged that most people tolerate is its own kind of harm, and there is no honest way to guess a portion you did not state.

    The rest are categorical

    Allergens, FODMAP subtypes, oral allergy syndrome and the GI irritant mechanisms have no gram cutoff. They follow established classifications: the allergens EU law requires to be declared on a label, the Monash University FODMAP database, and the pollen groups behind oral allergy syndrome. Two allergens are tracked that no label has to mention — onion and garlic, and mushroom — because no label has to declare them, and both turn up often enough in practice to be worth ruling out. Lupin, which is declarable, is left out: it barely reaches a Swedish plate, and this food list is Swedish first. Refined carbohydrates works the same way — assigned by food type and processing rather than by carbohydrate content, so whole grains, legumes and vegetables are never tagged with it however carb-heavy they are.

    Histamine is categorical, but a food is tagged only where there is measured histamine, proteolytic ripening, long fermentation with an undefined flora, a freshness-dependent mechanism, or alcohol. Foods justified only by the "histamine liberator" idea are not tagged — a review of ten published low-histamine diets found 68% of the excluded foods had no detectable histamine, and the release mechanism has never been demonstrated in humans. That is why yogurt, kefir, strawberry and avocado carry no histamine tag here, even though most lists include them.

    DAO competitors is a modifier rather than a trigger. Putrescine and cadaverine occupy the enzyme that breaks histamine down, so they may slow its clearance — but a food carrying them without any histamine has nothing to compete with. The trait is reported only when the selection also contains histamine.

    FODMAPs: the amount decides whether a food counts

    FODMAPs are the one categorical family with a published amount, and it is coarser than a nutrient figure. Monash University tests a stated serving of a food and rates it low, moderate or high. There is no gram figure for how much fructan is in a slice of bread, so there is nothing to add up.

    What the serving is good for is a threshold. In a meal, a food weighed out at or under the serving Monash rates low is in the meal without being counted as loading it; over that serving, it counts. FODMAPs are then reported like every other categorical family — which types are present, from how many ingredients — with the weight deciding which ingredients count. Twenty-five grams of avocado drops out; fifty grams does not.

    Some foods have no low serving at any amount, onion and garlic being the usual ones, and they count whatever the weight. Others have no serving on file yet, and those count on the tag alone. Both are named under the sentence, so it is always clear which foods the threshold was applied to and which it was not.

    One thing a threshold cannot show: servings are rated one food at a time, so several foods each within their own serving can still add up in one meal. That is said in words rather than turned into a number, because turning it into a number would need grams the source does not publish.

    A lot at once

    A meal can be hard on the gut without anything in it being wrong. The meal builder therefore says when one component arrives in an amount, or at a concentration, that the gut is likely to notice. This is not a nutritional assessment: nothing here says a meal is unhealthy, and nothing here is a level to stay under.

    Two different things are measured, because two different mechanisms are involved. An amount matters for fat, protein and fiber — fat and protein leave the stomach slowly and slow it further, and fiber arrives in the colon as bulk to ferment. A concentration matters for sugar, which draws water into the bowel by osmosis: fifteen grams of sugar in a glass of squash and the same fifteen grams spread through a plate of food are not the same event. Fat is measured both ways, because it does not dissolve into the watery part of a meal — it travels as its own phase, and the same grams spread thinner behave more gently.

    Crossing one of these lines is not a fault, and most people cross several at an ordinary dinner without noticing anything. They matter in two situations: when someone is eating well over what they are used to, and when the gut is already sensitive or already restricted. Fiber is the clearest case — a consistently high fiber intake is rarely what causes trouble, while a sudden rise over the usual often is.

    Too little water is its own event

    One of those lines runs the other way, on too little rather than too much. Sugar draws water into the stomach and small bowel until what is there is dilute enough to move on. A meal that brings little water of its own takes it from the body instead, and the stretching is what is felt — dried fruit eaten with nothing to drink, or a dense sweet dough.

    A meal is reported as dry below 40g of water per 100g, and only when it also carries at least 20g of sugars. Dryness alone is not the mechanism: plain starch is dry and has little that pulls water in. It also means a drink alongside settles it, which is the honest shape of the thing — the same dried fruit with a glass of milk is not the same meal.

    Helpings, where there is no gram figure

    Two traits scale with how much is eaten but have no figure per 100g in this database: salicylates, which no food table measures, and lactose, where the source reports total sugars and the sugar in an apple is not lactose. A meal counts those in helpings instead — a helping being the standard serving of whichever food carries the trait.

    A helping is not a gram figure and not a multiple of anything. It holds at least the amount the tag is set by, and can hold well over it, so four helpings means four servings' worth of carrying food rather than four times a threshold. Everything else that depends on amount — fat, protein, fiber, alcohol, and the bile-stimulating load that follows from fat and protein — is reported in real grams, because the figures are there.

    How well supported is each trait?

    Traits are not equally well grounded, and the analysis says so. Every trait carries an evidence level on one of three steps:

    • Well established Human trials or a directly measured mechanism. Allergens, lactose, FODMAPs and histamine sit here.
    • Limited Real evidence, but thin, mixed, or one step removed from the symptom. Bile stimulation and refined carbohydrates sit here.
    • Preliminary Test-tube work, animal models or clinical experience only, with no human trials. Acetic acid, peel and skin, and DAO competition sit here.

    The level says how well the trait itself is grounded — not how strongly you will react to it. A well-established trait can be irrelevant for you, and a preliminary one can be your main problem. Each trait's level, with a sentence on what it rests on, appears with the trait in the results.

    Fat and protein are tracked passively in the background. They are rarely the direct cause of GI symptoms on their own, so rather than competing for a top spot in the ranking they appear as a side note when over 90% of your selection shares one — a signal that they may be worsening symptoms driven by something else.

    Data sources

    FODMAP subtypes are cross-referenced against the Monash University FODMAP database. Threshold-based traits (lactose, alcohol, fiber, fat, protein, bile stimulant) use standard nutrition data per 100g. Refined Carbohydrates is tagged by food type and processing rather than a database, loosely following the same logic as the NOVA food-processing classification. Allergens, GI irritants, cross-reactivity and alpha-gal draw on clinical literature and practice experience, and are reviewed for accuracy before being added.

    Histamine started from the SIGHI food list but is not taken from it directly. Every entry was checked against published measurements of biogenic amines in food and against whether a plausible mechanism actually applies. Roughly two thirds of the entries did not survive that check. DAO Competitors uses the same measurement data, tagging only foods with a published quantification of putrescine or cadaverine above 10 mg/kg — no extrapolation from one food to a similar one. Salicylates use Australian portion-level measurements, chosen over a European dataset because that ranking is the one that has been tested in a blinded dietary trial.

    Sources disagree more than is comfortable, and where they do, this tool says so rather than picking quietly. How Certain Is Any of This? sets out where the uncertainty sits and how it was handled.

    Fat, protein, fiber, sugar and alcohol figures are checked against Livsmedelsverket's food database, the Swedish Food Agency's national food composition data, food by food. Around 365 of the foods here have been through that check. The rest have no Swedish entry — European cheeses, imported sauces, the vegan substitutes — and take their figures from the Danish, American or French national tables instead, recorded per food. Data sources lists which is which, food by food.

    The food list is still growing, with new additions prioritizing foods common in European and Scandinavian diets.

    Limitations

    This is a reference database, not a diagnostic tool. It describes foods, not people: it knows nothing about anyone's dose tolerance, cooking methods, food combinations, or any of the rest of what decides what actually happens. A shared property is something to read further about, not a finding.
