Background

This tool is built by Andreas Rydström, a registered dietitian based in Uppsala, Sweden. It grew out of a recurring pattern seen in clinical practice: a patient reacts to a handful of seemingly unrelated foods, and the shared thread — a FODMAP subtype, a histamine load, a cross-reactive allergen — only becomes visible once someone lays every food's traits side by side.

Once you have opened the site it works offline, and it can be added to a phone or tablet home screen like an app — useful in a room with no reliable connection.

Why this tool exists

Food intolerance work is full of diagnoses missed simply because nobody thought to check — alpha-gal syndrome, oral allergy syndrome, secondary lactose intolerance from undiagnosed coeliac disease. Spotting the shared thread by hand, meal by meal, is slow and easy to get wrong; this tool automates that comparison in seconds, surfacing overlooked possibilities without replacing a proper clinical workup.

How the analysis works

Every food in the tool is tagged with the traits it carries — FODMAP subtypes, histamine content, allergens, GI irritant mechanisms, and more. When you select a group of foods, the tool counts how many of them share each trait and ranks traits by how common they are across your selection.

Traits are tagged one of two ways. Some use a fixed threshold per 100g, with a minimum typical serving size so foods only eaten in tiny amounts don't get flagged:

  • Lactose: over 1g/100g
  • Alcohol: over 0.5% ABV
  • Fiber: over 6g/100g (skipped if a typical serving is under 10g)
  • Fat: over 10g/100g (skipped if a typical serving is under 10g)
  • Protein: over 20g/100g (skipped if a typical serving is under 10g)

Bile Stimulant uses either the fat or protein threshold above, but raised higher — over 17.5g fat or over 20g protein per 100g, matching EU food-labeling "high fat"/"high protein" categories. That's why foods like sour cream, ground meat, and Greek yogurt are split into separate low-fat, medium-fat, and full-fat entries: the fat content genuinely changes whether the trait applies. Turmeric is the one exception, tagged for its known effect on bile release despite not meeting either threshold.

Whole flaxseed, chia and psyllium are tagged on their fiber alone. The seeds are small and tough enough to be swallowed intact, so most of the fat never leaves the shell — the fiber is what acts on the gut. Ground versions release everything and are tagged on their full content. Larger seeds like pumpkin, and any seed that turns brittle when roasted, do not qualify.

Salicylates use a threshold too, but per portion rather than per 100g: at least 1 mg of salicylic acid in a normal serving. Concentration alone would mislead — cumin measures 605 mg/kg, but a portion is 2g, which puts it below a serving of green peas.

Other traits have no numeric threshold — they're categorical, based on established classifications rather than a gram cutoff: allergens, oral allergy syndrome, GI irritants, and FODMAPs (Monash University classifications).

Histamine is categorical but not simply inherited from a published list. A food is tagged only where there is measured histamine, proteolytic ripening, long fermentation with an undefined flora, a freshness-dependent mechanism, or alcohol. Foods justified only by the "histamine liberator" idea are not tagged: a review of ten published low-histamine diets found that 68% of the excluded foods had no detectable histamine, and the release mechanism has never been demonstrated in humans. That is why yogurt, kefir, strawberry and avocado carry no histamine tag here even though most lists include them.

DAO Competitors is a modifier rather than a trigger. Putrescine and cadaverine occupy the same enzyme that breaks histamine down, so they may slow its clearance — but a food carrying them without any histamine has nothing to compete with. The trait is therefore only reported when the selection also contains histamine.

How well supported is each trait?

Traits are not equally well grounded, and the analysis says so. Every trait carries an evidence level on one of three steps:

  • Well established Human trials or a directly measured mechanism. Allergens, lactose, FODMAPs and histamine sit here.
  • Limited Real evidence, but thin, mixed, or one step removed from the symptom. Bile stimulation and refined carbohydrates sit here.
  • Preliminary Test-tube work, animal models or clinical experience only, with no human trials. Acetic acid, peel and skin, and DAO competition sit here.

The level says how well the trait itself is grounded — not how strongly you will react to it. A well-established trait can be irrelevant for you, and a preliminary one can be your main problem. Each trait's level, with a sentence on what it rests on, appears with the trait in the results.

Refined Carbohydrates works the same way: it's assigned by food type and processing — white bread, sugar, and other ultra-processed or refined-grain foods — not by carb content. Whole grains, legumes, and vegetables are never tagged with it, no matter how carb-heavy they are.

Fat and protein content are also tracked passively in the background: since they're rarely the direct cause of GI symptoms on their own, instead of competing for a top spot in the trait ranking, they only appear as a side note when extremely common across your selection (over 90% of foods) — a signal they may be worsening symptoms driven by something else.

Data sources

FODMAP subtypes are cross-referenced against the Monash University FODMAP database. Threshold-based traits (lactose, alcohol, fiber, fat, protein, bile stimulant) use standard nutrition data per 100g. Refined Carbohydrates is tagged by food type and processing rather than a database, loosely following the same logic as the NOVA food-processing classification. Allergens, GI irritants, cross-reactivity and alpha-gal draw on clinical literature and practice experience, and are reviewed for accuracy before being added.

Histamine started from the SIGHI food list but is not taken from it directly. Every entry was checked against published measurements of biogenic amines in food and against whether a plausible mechanism actually applies. Roughly two thirds of the entries did not survive that check. DAO Competitors uses the same measurement data, tagging only foods with a published quantification of putrescine or cadaverine above 10 mg/kg — no extrapolation from one food to a similar one. Salicylates use Australian portion-level measurements, chosen over a European dataset because that ranking is the one that has been tested in a blinded dietary trial.

Sources disagree more than is comfortable, and where they do, this tool says so rather than picking quietly. How Certain Is Any of This? sets out where the uncertainty sits and how it was handled.

Fat, protein, fiber, sugar and alcohol figures are checked against Livsmedelsverket's food database, the Swedish Food Agency's national food composition data, food by food. Around 230 of the foods here have been through that check; the rest have no Swedish entry and rest on published nutrition data instead. Data sources lists which is which, food by food.

The food list is still growing, with new additions prioritizing foods common in European and Scandinavian diets.

Limitations

This is a triage and pattern-recognition tool, not a diagnostic one. It can't account for an individual's dose tolerance, cooking methods, food combinations, or the many other variables that affect real-world reactions. Shared traits point toward hypotheses worth investigating with a qualified health care professional — they aren't a diagnosis on their own.
