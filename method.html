This page is the long version: every rule with the reasoning and the numbers behind it. For the short account of what the tool does, see How the analysis works.

Every food in the tool is tagged with the traits it carries — FODMAP subtypes, histamine content, allergens, GI irritant mechanisms, and more. When you select a group of foods, the tool counts how many of them share each trait and ranks traits by how common they are across your selection.

Traits are tagged one of two ways. Some use a threshold — an amount of the nutrient in one portion, rather than a figure per 100g:

  • Fat: 6.1g in a portion
  • Protein: 15g in a portion
  • Fiber: 6.1g in a portion
  • Lactose: 5g in a portion
  • Bile stimulant: 9.5g of fat in a portion, counting protein at a fifth of its weight
  • Alcohol: above 0.5% ABV

Alcohol is the exception and stays a concentration. 0.5% is the legal line for calling a drink alcohol-free, and a legal line does not scale with how much you pour.

Portion size

A threshold per 100g only means something once you know how much of the food goes on a plate. 17.5g of fat per 100g is about 4g in a slice of cheese and about 22g in a portion of mince — the same number, five times the effect. So every food carries a typical serving in grams, and falls into one of six portion bands:

  • Up to 7g — spices, salt, soy sauce, a knob of horseradish
  • 7–17g — oil, butter, a spoonful of seeds
  • 17–35g — a slice of cheese, a handful of nuts, dried fruit
  • 35–69g — a slice of bread, a bowl of berries, an egg, a pita
  • 69–112g — fruit, vegetables, mushrooms, a sausage
  • Over 112g — meat, fish, cooked grains, a glass of milk

Portions are set per food group rather than per food, so that foods eaten the same way get the same number: every fruit is 100g, every berry 50g, every cut of meat, fish and poultry 125g, potato and cooked grains 175g, root vegetables 80g, a slice of bread 40g. Seventeen sizes cover all 474 foods. Where one departs from its group there is a reason — half an avocado, one egg, a wine glass rather than a tumbler.

The boundaries are 7, 17, 35, 69 and 112g rather than round numbers, and that is deliberate. Portion sizes bunch up on household measures — a great many foods land on exactly 25, 30, 100 or 125g — so a boundary placed on one of those would split foods that are the same size as each other. Each cut sits at the widest point of a gap: no food is within 11% of any of them.

Six bands rather than four, so that the typical portions they describe climb evenly: 5, 15, 25, 50, 100 and 175g, each step under a doubling of the one before.

Foods in the smallest band need no special handling: 2g of cinnamon carries 1g of fiber against a 6g dose, so the arithmetic rules them out on its own. Alcohol still applies there, since it works by concentration rather than by dose.

Each food is measured at its own portion. The bands are there to explain the reasoning and to group foods on the data sources page — they do not enter the arithmetic. Fat and fiber both sit at 6.1g rather than 6g. Portion figures pile up on round numbers the same way portion sizes do: 2dl of 3% milk is exactly 6.00g of fat, and three dairy drinks land on it together. A dose sitting on a number that several common foods hit exactly makes the tag turn on measurement noise. 6.1 clears milk while leaving a slice of cheese, at 6.24g, on the other side.

Bile Stimulant takes a higher dose than the plain fat trait, because it is about a strong gallbladder contraction rather than a fat load in general. Protein releases the same hormone but far more weakly, so instead of a separate protein threshold it counts toward the same dose at a fifth of its weight:

fat + 0.2 × protein ≥ 9.5g in a portion

Protein therefore lowers the fat a food needs rather than qualifying it on its own. 125g of cooked turkey, at 9g of fat and 28g of protein, reaches the dose; 125g of chicken breast, at 2g of fat, does not. A separate protein threshold could not tell those two apart at any setting — anything low enough to catch the turkey also caught cod and tuna, which is the opposite of the advice anyone would give in gallbladder disease. Turmeric is the one exception, tagged for its known effect on bile release despite reaching no dose at all.

Whole flaxseed, chia and psyllium are tagged on their fiber alone. The seeds are small and tough enough to be swallowed intact, so most of the fat never leaves the shell — the fiber is what acts on the gut. Ground versions release everything and are tagged on their full content. Larger seeds like pumpkin, and any seed that turns brittle when roasted, do not qualify.

Salicylates use a threshold too, but per portion rather than per 100g: at least 1 mg of salicylic acid in a normal serving. Concentration alone would mislead — cumin measures 605 mg/kg, but a portion is 2g, which puts it below a serving of green peas.

Other traits have no numeric threshold — they're categorical, based on established classifications rather than a gram cutoff: allergens, oral allergy syndrome, GI irritants, and FODMAPs (Monash University classifications).

Allergens: the EU list, minus one, plus two

The allergen list follows EU labelling law: cereals containing gluten, milk, egg, fish, crustaceans, molluscs, peanut, tree nut, soy, sesame, celery, mustard and sulphites. It replaced the American "Big 9", which is the wrong list for a Swedish clinic — it has no celery, no mustard and no sulphites, and it folds crustaceans and molluscs into a single "shellfish" that the EU deliberately splits, because the two tropomyosins differ enough that one group is often tolerated when the other is not.

Lupin is the fourteenth on the EU list and is not tracked. It is declarable everywhere in the union, but it is a Mediterranean legume that barely reaches a Swedish plate, and this database is Swedish first: a checkbox with no realistic food behind it costs a slot in the filter and returns nothing. If lupin flour becomes common in gluten-free baking here, it goes back in.

Two allergens are tracked that no label has to mention: onion and garlic and mushroom. Precisely because they are not declarable, nobody who reacts to them gets a warning, and they are the two that come up most often in practice outside the statutory list. Both sit at limited evidence rather than well established — the onion one because the overlap with fructan intolerance makes the two genuinely hard to separate, the mushroom one because most of what is documented concerns spore inhalation rather than eating them.

There is no broad "allergen" umbrella above the sixteen. There used to be, from before the specific allergens existed; once every one of them had its own tag the umbrella only took a top slot in the ranking without saying anything the specific tags did not already say. A selection of milk, egg and wheat foods now ranks those three, rather than ranking "allergen" first and burying them underneath.

Two consequences worth stating. Gluten cereals is one trait, as in the EU list, so oats, rye and barley products carry it alongside wheat — a rye crispbread is tagged where before it was not. And sulphites are not proteins, so the reaction is not a true allergy; the trait is applied where the declaration threshold of 10 mg/kg is normally exceeded, which in this database means wine, cider, balsamic vinegar and the light-coloured dried fruits that keep their colour.

Histamine is categorical but not simply inherited from a published list. A food is tagged only where there is measured histamine, proteolytic ripening, long fermentation with an undefined flora, a freshness-dependent mechanism, or alcohol. Foods justified only by the "histamine liberator" idea are not tagged: a review of ten published low-histamine diets found that 68% of the excluded foods had no detectable histamine, and the release mechanism has never been demonstrated in humans. That is why yogurt, kefir, strawberry and avocado carry no histamine tag here even though most lists include them.

DAO Competitors is a modifier rather than a trigger. Putrescine and cadaverine occupy the same enzyme that breaks histamine down, so they may slow its clearance — but a food carrying them without any histamine has nothing to compete with. The trait is therefore only reported when the selection also contains histamine.

How well supported is each trait?

Traits are not equally well grounded, and the analysis says so. Every trait carries an evidence level on one of three steps:

  • Well established Human trials or a directly measured mechanism. Allergens, lactose, FODMAPs and histamine sit here.
  • Limited Real evidence, but thin, mixed, or one step removed from the symptom. Bile stimulation and refined carbohydrates sit here.
  • Preliminary Test-tube work, animal models or clinical experience only, with no human trials. Acetic acid, peel and skin, and DAO competition sit here.

The level says how well the trait itself is grounded — not how strongly you will react to it. A well-established trait can be irrelevant for you, and a preliminary one can be your main problem. Each trait's level, with a sentence on what it rests on, appears with the trait in the results.

Refined Carbohydrates works the same way: it's assigned by food type and processing — white bread, sugar, and other ultra-processed or refined-grain foods — not by carb content. Whole grains, legumes, and vegetables are never tagged with it, no matter how carb-heavy they are.

Fat and protein content are also tracked passively in the background: since they're rarely the direct cause of GI symptoms on their own, instead of competing for a top spot in the trait ranking, they only appear as a side note when extremely common across your selection (over 90% of foods) — a signal they may be worsening symptoms driven by something else.
