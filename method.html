    Thresholds and portions

    This page is the long version: every rule with the reasoning and the numbers behind it. For the short account of what the tool does, see How the analysis works.

    Two kinds of property

    Every food in the tool is tagged with the traits it carries — FODMAP subtypes, histamine content, allergens, GI irritant mechanisms, and more. When you select a group of foods, the tool counts how many of them share each trait and ranks traits by how common they are across your selection.

    Traits are tagged one of two ways. Some use a threshold — an amount of the nutrient in one portion, rather than a figure per 100g:

    • Fat: 6.1g in a portion
    • Protein: 15g in a portion
    • Fiber: 6.1g in a portion
    • Lactose: 5g in a portion
    • Bile stimulant: 9.5g of fat in a portion, counting protein at a fifth of its weight
    • Alcohol: above 0.5% ABV

    Alcohol is the exception and stays a concentration. 0.5% is the legal line for calling a drink alcohol-free, and a legal line does not scale with how much you pour.

    Which figure is taken

    Every threshold above is grams of something, so every food needs a figure to be measured against. Which tables those come from, and in what order, is on Sources. This page is about what happens once a figure exists: which one is taken, and when none is.

    What a figure has to arrive with

    A food has to arrive with fat, protein, carbohydrate and water to be taken at all, even when the rest survives. That is not tidiness: a food with no figures is set aside from a meal entirely, while a food with some figures puts its whole weight into the denominator of every concentration and nothing into the numerator, so the meal reads leaner and drier than it is. Where a figure is missing anyway, the meal builder bounds it rather than assuming it — a food cannot hold more of a nutrient than it weighs — and reports a line only when both ends of that bound agree. Where they do not, it says nothing, which is the honest answer.

    Fibre is two different measurements

    One column is worth singling out. Ciqual publishes INFOODS tagnames for its columns, and the one against fibre is FIB-: in that system the suffix names the method — FIBTG for gravimetric AOAC, FIBTS for the sum of measured components — and a bare tag means it is unspecified. So whether resistant starch is counted in a French fibre figure cannot be known from the table. Given that the two methods differ by a third of the fiber dose and that the gap falls on starch, that is the one column where France is the weakest source of the four, and another reason it sits where it does.

    France sits above America and below Denmark for a reason worth stating. It goes above America because where the two overlap, the American set is describing a different shelf. It stays below Denmark because Ciqual publishes no confidence code per figure: it has to be taken on the table's standing, the way the Swedish and Danish tables are, while every American figure can be tested one at a time. A source that can be checked more closely is not automatically better — but between two tables of equal standing, the one describing the food you actually buy wins.

    Per figure, not per source

    SR Legacy is not uniformly laboratory-analysed, and the difference is written into the file. Every figure in USDA's data records how it was arrived at, and SR Legacy's opening entries include biscuits and a coating mix whose figures are marked manufacturer-supplied or back-calculated from the label — exactly what the Branded Foods set is rejected for.

    So the rule is applied per figure instead of per source. A number is taken if it was measured; if it is one of the two calculations every national table performs by definition — carbohydrate by difference, protein from nitrogen; or if the table itself records the nutrient as not naturally present, which is a fact about the food rather than a gap. A number supplied by a manufacturer, read off a label, estimated from an ingredient list or a recipe, or carried over from another form of the same food is dropped, and the food keeps whatever survives. That last one matters more here than it sounds: every wrong match this project has ever had was a form mismatch, so a figure that is itself a form substitution is the last thing to let in quietly. Nothing is averaged and nothing is filled in. On the Pillsbury cinnamon rolls that leaves no figures at all, which is the right answer; on a waffle measured in a laboratory it leaves all of them.

    Why only four tables

    Short on purpose. Every source added mixes another set of laboratory methods into the same column, and fiber does not survive that unnoticed: two standard methods in current use can differ by a couple of grams on the same food, which is a third of the fiber dose.

    That is measured, not assumed. USDA's Foundation Foods set runs both methods on some of the same samples — 17 foods carry AOAC 991.43 and AOAC 2011.25 side by side — and the newer method reads higher on all seventeen, by a median of 1.8g per 100g. That median is 30% of our fiber dose, which is where the "third of the dose" above comes from. The gap is not noise, and it is not evenly spread: it falls on starch. White rice goes from 0.15g to 2.77g, potato flour from 5.4g to 16.6g, a ripe banana from 1.7g to 4.6g — resistant starch is precisely what the newer method counts and the older one misses. So mixing sources does not blur fiber randomly; it systematically under-reads the starchy foods, which are the ones where the fiber question is usually being asked.

    Two things follow. The American Branded Foods set is not used at all — it is manufacturer label data, rounded to legal tolerances rather than measured. And a food with no figure from any of these gets none: the meal builder will not accept it, rather than counting it as a zero.

    Portion size

    A threshold per 100g only means something once you know how much of the food goes on a plate. 17.5g of fat per 100g is about 4g in a slice of cheese and about 22g in a portion of mince — the same number, five times the effect. So every food carries a typical serving in grams, and falls into one of six portion bands:

    • Up to 7g — spices, salt, soy sauce, a knob of horseradish
    • 7–17g — oil, butter, a spoonful of seeds
    • 17–35g — a slice of cheese, a handful of nuts, dried fruit
    • 35–69g — a slice of bread, a bowl of berries, an egg, a pita
    • 69–112g — fruit, vegetables, mushrooms, a sausage
    • Over 112g — meat, fish, cooked grains, a glass of milk

    Portions are set per food group rather than per food, so that foods eaten the same way get the same number: every fruit is 100g, every berry 50g, every cut of meat, fish and poultry 125g, potato and cooked grains 175g, root vegetables 80g, a slice of bread 40g. Seventeen sizes cover all 582 foods. Where one departs from its group there is a reason — half an avocado, one egg, a wine glass rather than a tumbler.

    What the standard serving cannot know

    A standard serving is what a typical plate holds, and a real plate often holds something else. Everything measured as an amount inherits that: eat half a portion and a trait shown here may not reach you, eat two and one that stays hidden may. The tool systematically underestimates the person who eats more than average, which is not a small group.

    The obvious fix is to tighten the doses — set fat at 4g instead of 6.1g so that the double helping still lands inside the flag. That is rejected, for three reasons. It breaks what makes the numbers defensible: today every dose traces to an amount in a serving you can name, and a padded dose answers "one serving, or maybe one and a half", which is not a question anyone can check. It flags foods most people tolerate, and in a population already inclined to restrict, a false alarm costs something real. And the problem it solves is better solved elsewhere: a meal built from stated weights knows what was eaten and does not have to guess.

    So the doses stay where the evidence puts them, and the limitation is stated instead of hidden — in the analysis itself, not only here.

    Where the boundaries sit

    The boundaries are 7, 17, 35, 69 and 112g rather than round numbers, and that is deliberate. Portion sizes bunch up on household measures — a great many foods land on exactly 25, 30, 100 or 125g — so a boundary placed on one of those would split foods that are the same size as each other. Each cut sits at the widest point of a gap: no food is within 11% of any of them.

    Six bands rather than four, so that the typical portions they describe climb evenly: 5, 15, 25, 50, 100 and 175g, each step under a doubling of the one before.

    Foods in the smallest band need no special handling: 2g of cinnamon carries 1g of fiber against a 6g dose, so the arithmetic rules them out on its own. Alcohol still applies there, since it works by concentration rather than by dose.

    Each food is measured at its own portion. The bands are there to explain the reasoning and to group foods on the data sources page — they do not enter the arithmetic. What they do show is how much the same dose demands of a food depending on how much of it goes on a plate. Taking a typical portion from each band:

    Fat and fiber both sit at 6.1g rather than 6g. Portion figures pile up on round numbers the same way portion sizes do: 2dl of 3% milk is exactly 6.00g of fat, and three dairy drinks land on it together. A dose sitting on a number that several common foods hit exactly makes the tag turn on measurement noise. 6.1 clears milk while leaving a slice of cheese, at 6.24g, on the other side.

    Bile Stimulant takes a higher dose than the plain fat trait, because it is about a strong gallbladder contraction rather than a fat load in general. Protein releases the same hormone but far more weakly, so instead of a separate protein threshold it counts toward the same dose at a fifth of its weight:

    fat + 0.2 × protein ≥ 9.5g in a portion

    Protein therefore lowers the fat a food needs rather than qualifying it on its own. 125g of cooked turkey, at 9g of fat and 28g of protein, reaches the dose; 125g of chicken breast, at 2g of fat, does not. A separate protein threshold could not tell those two apart at any setting — anything low enough to catch the turkey also caught cod and tuna, which is the opposite of the advice anyone would give in gallbladder disease. Turmeric is the one exception, tagged for its known effect on bile release despite reaching no dose at all.

    Whole flaxseed, chia and psyllium are tagged on their fiber alone. The seeds are small and tough enough to be swallowed intact, so most of the fat never leaves the shell — the fiber is what acts on the gut. Ground versions release everything and are tagged on their full content. Larger seeds like pumpkin, and any seed that turns brittle when roasted, do not qualify.

    Salicylates use a threshold too, but per portion rather than per 100g: at least 1 mg of salicylic acid in a normal serving. Concentration alone would mislead — cumin measures 605 mg/kg, but a portion is 2g, which puts it below a serving of green peas.

    Other traits have no numeric threshold — they're categorical, based on established classifications rather than a gram cutoff: allergens, oral allergy syndrome, GI irritants, and FODMAPs (Monash University classifications).

    FODMAPs

    The split above — a dose where a dose exists, plain presence where it does not — put FODMAPs on the categorical side, and that is where they stay. But they are the one categorical family with a published amount, so it is worth being exact about what the amount is. Monash tests a stated serving of a food and rates it low, moderate or high. It does not publish grams of fructan per 100g. A traffic light against a serving size is what exists.

    The first attempt treated the low serving as a unit and divided by it: 100g of apple against a 20g low serving is five servings, and servings from different foods add up. The arithmetic is defensible and the output was not. It produced figures like "polyols 6.7" on an ordinary plate — a number carrying two decimal places, an implied common scale between fructans and polyols, and an implied linearity, none of which the source supports. On top of that the table is partial, so every total was a floor with holes in it, and a floor with holes is a poor thing to print as a number. It was dressing a traffic light up as a measurement.

    What the serving honestly supports is a threshold. At or under the serving rated low, this food is not what is loading the meal; over it, it is. So the amount decides whether an ingredient counts, not how much, and FODMAPs are then reported exactly like allergens and the irritants: which types are present, from how many ingredients. That also means one presentation to learn instead of two, and the threshold still does the work you want — 25g of avocado drops out of the sentence, 50g does not, and leaving the onion out changes the line.

    Three cases are named rather than folded in. A food with no low serving at any amount, onion and garlic being the usual ones, counts whatever the weight. A food with no serving on file counts on its tag alone, and is named so it is clear the threshold was not applied to it. A food over its serving counts towards every subtype it carries, which overstates whichever one was not limiting — the serving was set by the limiting one and the traffic light does not say which. That last overstatement is the price of the source, and it is smaller here than it was under the sum: it now only reaches as far as calling a subtype present, which is what this section does to every categorical trait anyway.

    What the threshold cannot show is stacking, and stacking is real: servings are rated one food at a time, so several foods each within their own serving can still add up. That is printed as a sentence under the FODMAP line rather than as a number, because a number would need the grams the source does not give.

    The figures live in fodmap-data.js, hand-entered with a source, in the same spirit as nutrition-manual.js. There is no bulk export from Monash, so this table will always be typed in rather than built, and it will always be partial before it is complete. It covers about a third of the FODMAP foods that can go in a meal, and every figure still needs checking against the current app: serving sizes are revised as foods are re-tested, and a stale figure looks exactly like a fresh one. Asparagus was corrected against the app in the same sitting — it is tagged for excess fructose now, not fructans.

    The meal, not the food

    Every threshold above is a property of one food in one portion. A meal is a different unit, and the single-food doses cannot be reused on it — a normal mixed dinner holds 20–30g of fat, so the 6.1g that flags a food would flag every meal ever built. That is not a case for a more lenient number; it is a case for measuring something else.

    What a meal is measured on

    What is worth measuring is narrower than it first looks. This is not a nutritional assessment and must not become one — the question is not whether a meal is good, but whether one component arrives in an amount, or at a concentration, that the gut is likely to notice. Those are different questions and only the second one is ours.

    So two units, for two mechanisms. An amount, for fat, protein and fiber. A concentration per 100g of meal, for sugar — osmolality is a property of the solution and not of the total, so 15g of sugar in a glass and the same 15g through a plate of food are different events, and only the first is hypertonic. Fat appears under both, because it is hydrophobic: it travels as its own phase rather than dissolving into the watery one, so a meal that is a sixth fat by weight behaves differently from one where the same fat is spread thin.

    • Fat: 25g in the meal — where low-fat advice for fat malabsorption puts a single meal
    • Fat, as a share: 15g per 100g of meal
    • Sugars, as a share: 10g per 100g of meal — roughly the line sports drinks are formulated to stay under, above which a solution is hypertonic
    • Fiber: 10g in the meal — about a third of a day's fiber arriving at once
    • Protein: 60g in the meal

    Protein started at 40g and was moved. At 40g an ordinary plate of chicken, rice and carrot tripped it, and a line that fires on an ordinary dinner teaches people to skip the section — which costs the lines that are worth reading. Protein is also the weakest of these mechanisms, so it should be the last one to speak. 60g is well past a normal main course.

    The wording carries as much weight as the numbers here, and it has one job: to stop a figure on a screen from reading as a limit. These lines are not levels to stay under and crossing one is not a fault. They matter when someone is eating well over what they are used to, and when the gut is already sensitive or already restricted. Fiber makes the point plainest — a consistently high intake is rarely what causes trouble, and a sudden rise over the usual usually is — but it is true of all of them, and the tool cannot know anyone's usual. Nothing is said at all when nothing crosses a line: a section that always has something to report is a section nobody reads.

    Concentrations are worked out over the weight the figures actually cover, not the weight of the plate, because a food with no figures contributes grams but no sugar and would water every concentration down. Where a meal is only part-covered, a line crossed is still crossed — the real figure is higher — but a line not crossed settles nothing, and the page says so.

    Too little water, not too much of something

    One line runs the other way. Osmolality is solutes per unit water, so a dry meal and a sweet meal are the same event seen from two sides: eat 400g of raisins with nothing to drink and the stomach has to secrete the water the meal did not bring before anything can move on. That water comes from the body, and the distension is what is felt. A dense sweet dough is the same thing in a worse form.

    So the meal builder reports a meal below 40g of water per 100g — but only when it also carries at least 20g of sugars. Dryness on its own is not the mechanism: a plate of plain starch is dry and has little that is osmotically active until it is digested, and without the second condition the line would only be saying that bread is dry. It also means a drink alongside clears it, which is the point — 100g of raisins on their own is flagged and the same raisins with a glass of milk are not.

    It needs a water figure for every food in the meal, or it stays quiet. One food short and the meal looks drier than it is: the missing food puts weight in the denominator and no water in the numerator, which is the direction that raises a false alarm. Water was briefly derived as 100 minus everything else instead — that lands within a couple of grams on most foods and twelve out on raisins, which is precisely the food it would have been used on. The column is read from the source now.

    What these lines rest on, one at a time

    They are not equally well founded, and lumping them together would hide that.

    • Osmolality slows gastric emptying, and water is drawn into the lumen to dilute a hypertonic load. Textbook physiology, from the duodenal osmoreceptor work of the 1970s onward. Not in doubt.
    • Carbohydrate above roughly 8–10% by weight delays gastric emptying and provokes GI symptoms. Replicated many times in exercise physiology, which is where the sugar line comes from — it is why sports drinks are formulated where they are.
    • Fat delays gastric emptying and releases CCK. Well established. The 25g line is not from a trial; it is where low-fat advice for fat malabsorption puts a single meal.
    • A dry meal as a symptom trigger in its own right. The mechanism follows from the first point, but nobody has run meal water content as an independent variable. This line is physiological reasoning plus clinical experience, and it should be read that way.
    • The fiber and protein lines are practical, not derived. A third of a day's fiber at once, and a protein load past a normal main course.

    One caution that belongs here rather than on the page, because it cuts against the obvious reading. In dumping syndrome the standing advice is to separate fluids from solids, since fluid speeds a hyperosmolar load out of the stomach and into the jejunum, which is where those symptoms come from. "Drink something with it" is therefore right for one problem and wrong for another. That is why every line in this section states a mechanism and stops: none of them is advice, and none of them tells anyone what to do about it.

    Helpings: kept for two traits, dropped for the rest

    Before the nutrient figures existed, a meal reported every amount-based trait as a count of standard servings of tagged food, in a table: Fat — 2 — 2 ingredients. It could not be read without the paragraph explaining it, and the number invited exactly the wrong reading. 2 looks like twice the threshold. It is not: it means the plate holds two standard servings of fat-tagged food, each of which is at least the dose and may be far over it. Two helpings can be 13g of fat or 80g, and the table could not tell those apart.

    Most of it is now unnecessary. Fat, protein, fiber and alcohol are real grams in the meal's own table, and the bile-stimulating load can be derived there too — fat counting protein at a fifth of its weight, the same rule single foods are tagged by, applied to the whole plate. A gram figure beats a proxy that needs a paragraph.

    Two traits have no gram figure and cannot get one from this data. Salicylates are not measured in any food composition table we use. Lactose is reported as total sugars, and the sugar in an apple is not lactose — the same limitation the soft marker in the data check exists for. Sweden publishes no lactose column at all, so no Swedish round can ever fix that; where Denmark or France has been matched to the same food, that one column is borrowed and the food's own line records it, but that is a food at a time rather than a solution. For those two, helpings are the only quantity there is, so they stay — as a sentence that names the unit, not a bare number in a column, and with the wording leaning harder as the count climbs: part of one standard helping, about one, a figure, then a lot in one sitting past four and several times what one helping carries past six.

    The table is not to come back. If a trait gains a gram figure, it moves up into the grams; if a new one arrives without, it gets a sentence.

    Allergens

    The allergen list follows EU labelling law: cereals containing gluten, milk, egg, fish, crustaceans, molluscs, peanut, tree nut, soy, sesame, celery and mustard. It replaced the American "Big 9", which is the wrong list for a Swedish clinic — it has no celery and no mustard, and it folds crustaceans and molluscs into a single "shellfish" that the EU deliberately splits, because the two tropomyosins differ enough that one group is often tolerated when the other is not.

    Lupin is the fourteenth on the EU list and is not tracked. It is declarable everywhere in the union, but it is a Mediterranean legume that barely reaches a Swedish plate, and this database is Swedish first: a checkbox with no realistic food behind it costs a slot in the filter and returns nothing. If lupin flour becomes common in gluten-free baking here, it goes back in.

    Two allergens are tracked that no label has to mention: onion and garlic and mushroom. Precisely because they are not declarable, nobody who reacts to them gets a warning, and they are the two that come up most often in practice outside the statutory list. Both sit at limited evidence rather than well established — the onion one because the overlap with fructan intolerance makes the two genuinely hard to separate, the mushroom one because most of what is documented concerns spore inhalation rather than eating them.

    No umbrella above the sixteen

    There is no broad "allergen" umbrella above the sixteen. There used to be, from before the specific allergens existed; once every one of them had its own tag the umbrella only took a top slot in the ranking without saying anything the specific tags did not already say. A selection of milk, egg and wheat foods now ranks those three, rather than ranking "allergen" first and burying them underneath.

    One consequence worth stating: gluten cereals is a single trait, as in the EU list, so oats, rye and barley products carry it alongside wheat — a rye crispbread is tagged where before it was not.

    Sulphites are not in the allergen card

    Sulphites are the fourteenth item on the EU list and are tracked, but they sit with the other digestive factors rather than among the allergens. They are a preservative, not a protein: nothing about the reaction is immune-mediated, and the best documented effect is bronchoconstriction in people with asthma. Filing them beside milk and peanut would have said something about the mechanism that is not true. They keep the allergens article for their write-up, since that is where the labelling rule is explained.

    The tag is applied where the 10 mg/kg declaration threshold is normally exceeded, which in this database means wine, cider, balsamic vinegar and the light-coloured dried fruits that keep their colour.

    Properties with no gram threshold

    Histamine, DAO competitors, salicylates, pollen cross-reactivity and alpha-gal have no amount to be measured against. They are tagged from food type and established classifications rather than from a figure.

    Histamine is categorical but not simply inherited from a published list. A food is tagged only where there is measured histamine, proteolytic ripening, long fermentation with an undefined flora, a freshness-dependent mechanism, or alcohol. Foods justified only by the "histamine liberator" idea are not tagged: a review of ten published low-histamine diets found that 68% of the excluded foods had no detectable histamine, and the release mechanism has never been demonstrated in humans. That is why yogurt, kefir, strawberry and avocado carry no histamine tag here even though most lists include them.

    DAO Competitors is a modifier rather than a trigger. Putrescine and cadaverine occupy the same enzyme that breaks histamine down, so they may slow its clearance — but a food carrying them without any histamine has nothing to compete with. The trait is therefore only reported when the selection also contains histamine.

    How well established is each property?

    Traits are not equally well grounded, and the analysis says so. Every trait carries an evidence level on one of three steps:

    • Well established Human trials or a directly measured mechanism. Allergens, lactose, FODMAPs and histamine sit here.
    • Limited Real evidence, but thin, mixed, or one step removed from the symptom. Bile stimulation and refined carbohydrates sit here.
    • Preliminary Test-tube work, animal models or clinical experience only, with no human trials. Acetic acid, peel and skin, and DAO competition sit here.

    The level says how well the trait itself is grounded — not how strongly you will react to it. A well-established trait can be irrelevant for you, and a preliminary one can be your main problem. Each trait's level, with a sentence on what it rests on, appears with the trait in the results.

    Refined Carbohydrates works the same way: it's assigned by food type and processing — white bread, sugar, and other ultra-processed or refined-grain foods — not by carb content. Whole grains, legumes, and vegetables are never tagged with it, no matter how carb-heavy they are.

    Fat and protein content are also tracked passively in the background: since they're rarely the direct cause of GI symptoms on their own, instead of competing for a top spot in the trait ranking, they only appear as a side note when extremely common across your selection (over 90% of foods) — a signal they may be worsening symptoms driven by something else.
